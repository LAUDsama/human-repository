How to Make Oral Rehydration Solution

The exact, humble mixture of clean water, salt, and sugar that helps the gut pull fluid back into the body before dehydration becomes collapse.

01What Rehydration Was

Rehydration was the work of returning water and salts to a body losing them faster than ordinary thirst could replace them.

Humans often noticed dehydration before they had a scientific name for it. A sick child grew quiet. An old person became confused in the heat. A traveler stopped sweating. A laborer cramped after hours under the sun. A person with diarrhea became weak not because the bowels were embarrassing, but because water, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, and other salts were leaving the body in dangerous amounts. The body could survive hunger for a while. It could not survive severe fluid loss for long.

Oral rehydration solution, usually called ORS, is one of the plainest and most important medical technologies humans developed. It is not glamorous. It is not a cure for infection, poison, heatstroke, shock, kidney failure, sepsis, cholera, dysentery, or every kind of vomiting. It is a carefully proportioned drink that lets the small intestine absorb water efficiently even during many diarrheal illnesses. Used early and correctly, it can keep mild dehydration from becoming severe and can help treat mild to moderate dehydration while medical help is arranged or when it is not immediately reachable.

The humility of ORS matters. It works because it respects measurement. More salt does not make it stronger; it can make it dangerous. More sugar does not make it more nourishing; it can worsen diarrhea by pulling water into the intestine. Dirty water does not become safe because salt and sugar were added. ORS is a small agreement with biology: the right substances, in the right amount, in clean water, given patiently by mouth.

02The Deep History of Dehydration Treatment

Before modern fluid therapy, people treated fluid loss with whatever their traditions and landscapes offered: broths, rice water, gruels, teas, salted soups, fermented drinks, diluted fruit drinks, and continued breastfeeding. Some helped because they supplied water, salt, starch, or glucose. Some harmed because they were contaminated, too concentrated, too alcoholic, or given while food and breast milk were withheld. Much of the old work was observation. A caregiver learned to keep offering small amounts when a sick person could not tolerate a cup. A parent learned that a baby who stopped nursing, stopped urinating, or became limp was in danger.

Modern understanding developed through cholera, pediatric diarrhea, famine medicine, military medicine, and physiology. Cholera was especially brutal because it could empty a body rapidly through watery stool. In the 19th century, physicians learned that intravenous saline could temporarily revive patients, but IV therapy required sterile equipment, trained workers, and supplies. It could not be placed in every village, home, refugee camp, or road where dehydration struck.

The decisive insight was that the intestine was not simply broken during many diarrheal diseases. Even when toxins drove fluid into the bowel, the gut still retained transport systems that could absorb sodium and glucose together. If sodium and glucose arrived in the right relationship, water followed. By the late 20th century, oral rehydration therapy became a central public-health tool, especially for childhood diarrhea. WHO and UNICEF promoted packaged oral rehydration salts and later recommended a reduced-osmolarity formulation that improved effectiveness compared with older formulations.

The achievement was not only chemical. It was educational and logistical. Packets had to be manufactured, distributed, mixed with the correct volume of water, accepted by families, and used before the child became critically ill. Mothers, fathers, grandparents, community health workers, teachers, pharmacists, nurses, and neighbors all became part of the treatment. ORS showed that medicine can be powerful without being dramatic, and that a lifesaving intervention may fit in a paper sachet or a clean one-liter bottle.

03The Biology of Salt Sugar and Water

Water in the body is never just water. It moves with dissolved salts, proteins, sugars, acids, and other molecules. Sodium is especially important for the fluid outside cells. Potassium is especially important inside cells. The body keeps these concentrations within narrow ranges because nerves, muscles, blood pressure, kidney function, and consciousness depend on them. When diarrhea, vomiting, fever, sweating, burns, or poor intake remove fluid, the danger is not only thirst. It is shrinking blood volume, poor circulation, electrolyte disturbance, acid-base disturbance, and eventually shock.

The small intestine has transporters that move sodium and glucose from the gut into the body together. This coupled transport remains functional in many diarrheal illnesses. When sodium and glucose are absorbed, water follows by osmosis. ORS uses this pathway. It does not plug the bowel. It does not kill germs. It does not make every stool normal. It gives the body a route for absorbing what it is losing.

This is why the proportions are serious. A drink that is only water may help thirst, but large amounts of plain water without salts can fail to replace sodium losses and, in some circumstances, contribute to low blood sodium. A drink that is too salty can raise sodium dangerously. A drink that is too sugary can draw water into the gut and worsen stool losses. Soft drinks, undiluted fruit juice, sports drinks, energy drinks, and sweet teas are not the same as ORS. Some contain too much sugar, not enough sodium, caffeine, or other ingredients that do not match dehydration treatment.

Core principle ORS works because clean water, sodium, and glucose are present in a narrow useful balance. Treat the recipe as medicine, not as a beverage adjusted by taste.

04Rehydration Across Contexts

Diarrhea is the classic setting for ORS, but dehydration has many faces. A toddler with viral gastroenteritis, an adult with foodborne diarrhea, a worker sweating in heat, a person vomiting after illness, and an elder eating and drinking poorly during fever can all need fluid replacement. The cause matters because some causes require more than ORS. Bloody diarrhea may mean invasive infection. Green bile vomit can signal obstruction. Confusion, severe weakness, fainting, or cold mottled skin can mean shock. Heat illness can become heatstroke, which is an emergency requiring cooling and urgent care.

Context also changes what is practical. In a clinic, a child with mild to moderate dehydration can be weighed, examined, given measured ORS, reassessed, and moved to IV or nasogastric fluids if oral intake fails. In a home, the work may be less exact: offer small frequent sips, continue breastfeeding, watch urine, and seek help early if danger signs appear. In a refugee camp, public-health teams must combine ORS distribution with safe water, sanitation, cholera control, vaccination where applicable, nutrition, and rapid referral. In an isolated household, the first challenge may be making the water safe before any solution is mixed.

Cultural habits matter too. Many families have trusted drinks for illness: soup, rice water, coconut water, tea, lemon water, or diluted porridge. Some may be useful as food or comfort after rehydration begins, but they should not be confused with measured ORS unless prepared according to a tested formula. The respectful approach is not to mock tradition. It is to teach the danger of wrong concentration and to preserve what helps: continued feeding, careful observation, breastfeeding, and patient caregiving.

05What Medicine Learned

Medicine learned that dehydration kills quietly and quickly, especially in infants and young children. It learned that children are not just small adults. They have smaller reserves, higher metabolic needs, and depend on caregivers for every drink. An infant can become dehydrated faster than an older child. A child who is sleepy, limp, inconsolable, breathing abnormally, not making tears, not urinating, or refusing fluids should not be treated as a routine stomach bug.

Medicine also learned that oral therapy can be powerful. For many mild to moderate cases, ORS by mouth can work as well as more invasive care when the person can drink and is monitored. Vomiting does not automatically make ORS impossible. Tiny amounts, such as a teaspoon or 5 milliliters every few minutes, may be tolerated when larger swallows come back up. As dehydration improves, vomiting may lessen. But oral therapy has limits. A person in shock, unable to protect the airway, unconscious, severely lethargic, persistently unable to drink, or worsening despite adequate ORS needs urgent medical care and may need IV or tube-delivered fluid.

Another lesson was nutritional. Older advice often told families to rest the gut, stop feeding, dilute formula, or give only clear liquids for too long. Modern guidance generally emphasizes continued breastfeeding, return to age-appropriate feeding as soon as tolerated, and avoidance of highly sugary drinks. The bowel heals with nourishment. In many settings, zinc supplementation is recommended for children with diarrhea, but zinc is not a substitute for fluid replacement and should follow local medical guidance.

06The 21st-Century Collapse Risk

The 21st century made rehydration knowledge both widespread and fragile. Packets of ORS could be ordered, shipped, stocked in clinics, and included in disaster supplies. Search engines could display recipes instantly. Pharmacies sold commercial oral electrolyte solutions. Hospitals had IV fluids, pumps, labs, and pediatric protocols. At the same time, many people became dependent on systems they did not understand: safe municipal water, stable supply chains, refrigeration, pharmacies, emergency departments, and internet access.

A storm, flood, blackout, war, epidemic, contaminated water system, or supply shortage can turn diarrhea into a community crisis. The risk is not only that people lack packets. It is that they may mix packets with the wrong amount of water, use contaminated water, ration ORS incorrectly, replace it with soda, or delay care until a child is already severely dehydrated. Online recipes can also be dangerous when copied carelessly. A rounded spoon is not a level spoon. A kitchen teaspoon used for eating may not equal a measuring teaspoon. A half-liter bottle is not a liter. Salt substitutes are not table salt unless a medical recipe specifically calls for them.

The collapse risk is educational. Communities need ordinary people who know how to store ORS packets, read labels, measure a liter, boil and cool water when necessary, recognize danger signs, and teach the method without improvising. They also need sanitation: latrines, handwashing, clean water storage, safe food, isolation during outbreaks, and waste control. ORS saves lives after fluid loss begins. Public health tries to prevent the losses from multiplying through a whole neighborhood.

07How to Practice Oral Rehydration

Use commercially prepared ORS packets when available. They are designed to be mixed with a specific volume of clean water, often 1 liter, but always read the packet. Do not split a packet unless the instructions allow exact division. Do not mix a packet into too little water to make it stronger. Do not add sugar, salt, juice, or flavoring unless medical instructions specifically permit it. Once mixed, keep the solution covered and clean, and discard it after 24 hours or sooner if contaminated.

Common home recipe If no ORS packet is available, a widely taught emergency recipe is 1 liter of clean water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar, and 1/2 level teaspoon of table salt, stirred until fully dissolved. Use real measuring spoons and an accurate one-liter container. If you cannot measure accurately, do not guess.
  1. Start with safe water. Use sealed safe water, properly treated water, or water that has been boiled and cooled if the source is uncertain. Do not mix ORS into water that may carry sewage or flood contamination unless it has been made safe.
  2. Use a clean container that holds exactly 1 liter. Mark a dedicated bottle if needed. Wash hands, container, and stirring tool before mixing.
  3. Add 6 level teaspoons of sugar. Level means flat across the top, not heaped. Add 1/2 level teaspoon of ordinary table salt. Stir until the solution is clear and dissolved.
  4. Taste only as a rough safety check. It should be no saltier than tears. Taste cannot replace measurement. If there is any doubt that too much salt was added, throw it away and make a new batch.
  5. Give small frequent amounts. For vomiting, try 5 milliliters, about 1 teaspoon, every few minutes and increase slowly as tolerated. For diarrhea, keep replacing losses and continue feeding when possible.
  6. Continue breastfeeding infants. Do not stop breast milk. Seek medical advice early for infants, especially young infants, because they can deteriorate quickly.
  7. Watch response. A person should become more alert, urinate more, have moistening mouth, improving thirst, and better strength. Worsening sleepiness, no urine, fast breathing, cold hands and feet, or inability to drink means the plan is failing.

ORS is usually given cool or at room temperature. It should be sipped, spooned, or syringed slowly rather than forced. For children, a spoon, medicine syringe, or small cup often works better than a large bottle. If a child vomits, pause briefly and restart with smaller amounts. Do not use antidiarrheal medicines in infants or young children unless a qualified clinician directs it; some can cause serious harm.

08Common Failure Modes

Wrong concentration. The most dangerous household failure is inaccurate measurement. Too much salt can cause hypernatremia, seizures, or worse. Too much sugar can worsen diarrhea. Too little salt may fail to replace losses. Use level measuring spoons and a true liter.

Dirty water. ORS made with unsafe water can keep introducing the organisms that caused the illness. Boiling and cooling, filtering plus disinfection where appropriate, or using sealed safe water may matter as much as the recipe.

Waiting too long. ORS works best early. Do not wait for a child to become limp, stop urinating, or develop sunken eyes before acting. Start with diarrhea and vomiting, and seek help promptly when risk is high.

Using the wrong drink. Soda, energy drinks, undiluted juice, sweet tea, alcohol, and many sports drinks are not ORS. They may contain too much sugar, too little sodium, caffeine, or alcohol. Broth and food can help nutrition, but they do not replace a measured solution when dehydration is the problem.

Missing danger signs. ORS is insufficient when there is severe dehydration, shock, altered consciousness, seizure, severe weakness, persistent inability to drink, suspected poisoning, heatstroke, severe abdominal pain, green bile vomit, blood or black stool, repeated vomiting that prevents intake, or signs of sepsis. Infants under 6 months, premature infants, medically fragile people, pregnant people, elders, and immunocompromised people deserve a lower threshold for medical evaluation.

Stopping nourishment unnecessarily. Continuing breastfeeding is important. Once vomiting eases, children should return to age-appropriate food as tolerated. Prolonged clear-liquid diets can weaken recovery.

09Teaching Children Hydration

Children should learn hydration as body literacy, not fear. Teach them that pee color, thirst, dizziness, dry mouth, headache, heat, and tiredness can be clues. Teach that water matters before play, during heat, after sweating, and during illness. Teach that diarrhea and vomiting are not shameful; they are signals to tell an adult early.

For older children, show the difference between ordinary drinking and medical rehydration. Let them see a one-liter bottle. Let them practice identifying a level teaspoon with dry sugar, not during an emergency. Explain that ORS is measured because the body needs balance. Too much salt is not brave. Too much sugar is not kind. Guessing is not helping.

For infants and young children, the teaching is mostly for caregivers. Danger signs include unusual sleepiness or limpness, difficult waking, no tears when crying, very dry mouth or tongue, sunken eyes, sunken soft spot in a baby, fast or deep breathing, cold or mottled hands and feet, very little urine, no wet diapers for many hours, persistent vomiting, blood in stool, fever with distress, refusal to drink, or a caregiver's sense that the child is getting worse. Fewer than 4 wet diapers in 24 hours for an infant, or much less urination than usual in an older child, should be taken seriously.

Children also learn from adult calm. A panicked adult may force large gulps and trigger vomiting. A steady adult offers a teaspoon, waits, offers again, watches urine, keeps notes, and asks for help early. Hydration is care repeated in small amounts.

10If Everything Broke

If systems failed, ORS knowledge would become part of household and community survival. Store sealed ORS packets where heat, rodents, moisture, and children cannot damage them. Rotate them before expiration. Keep a dedicated one-liter container, measuring spoons, soap, water-treatment supplies, and written instructions in plain language. Do not rely on memory alone. Emergencies make people tired and imprecise.

Community preparation should pair ORS with prevention. Protect wells. Separate latrines from water sources. Wash hands after toileting and before food. Dispose of stool safely, especially during diarrhea outbreaks. Keep flies away from food. Boil or disinfect questionable water. Feed sick people safely. Isolate contagious illness when needed. Record who is sick, how much they drink, how often they urinate, whether there is blood, whether fever is present, and whether mental status changes.

In a breakdown, triage matters. The quiet child in the corner may be in more danger than the crying child. The elder who becomes confused may be dehydrated, infected, overheated, or all three. The person who cannot sit up, cannot drink, or has cold clammy skin needs urgent help beyond a household recipe. Organize transport routes before crisis if possible. Know who has medical training. Know where clean water can be obtained. Know which families have infants, disabled members, pregnant people, or elders who will need early checking.

If packets run out and accurate measuring tools remain, the home recipe can help. If accurate measurement is impossible, safer action may be to give small amounts of clean water, continue breastfeeding, provide appropriate food or broth as tolerated, and seek skilled help rather than guessing salt into a cup. In collapse, precision is mercy.
