How to Fix Small Things

The human refusal to let every loose hinge, torn shirt, flat tire, missing screw, and worn shoe become trash before it has been understood.

01What Repair Was

Repair was the old conversation between a person and a thing that had stopped cooperating. Before replacement became the reflex, humans asked a quieter question: what failed here, and can it be brought back?

A button came loose. A chair rocked. A bicycle tire sagged against the rim. A door hinge complained. A lamp flickered because a cord had been bent too often. A shoe opened at the sole. None of these small failures needed to become a crisis. They were invitations to look closely. The thread had worn through. The screw had backed out. The tube had been pierced. The wood had swollen. The glue had dried. The object was not mysterious once attention had stayed with it long enough.

Repair was not only thrift, though thrift mattered. It was a way of remaining in relationship with the material world. To mend a shirt was to remember that cloth had grain, tension, edge, and history. To tighten a hinge was to learn that doors are held up by small pieces of metal bearing repeated weight. To patch a bike tire was to understand air as something trapped, pressured, and lost through a hole smaller than a seed. The repaired thing carried a lesson forward into the hand.

Repair also trained respect. A thing that could be fixed was not disposable in the mind. It had parts, labor, material, and use embedded in it. Someone cut the leather, spun the thread, forged the screw, molded the plastic, packed the bearing, wrote the manual, shipped the box, and used the object until it showed wear. Repair asked people to see this accumulation instead of treating every failure as permission to erase it.

02The Deep History of Repair

Repair is older than agriculture, cities, and writing. Stone tools were resharpened. Wooden handles were replaced. Nets were mended. Baskets were patched. Hides were stitched. Pottery was sometimes repaired with holes, ties, staples, resins, or other local methods when the vessel still had value. A tool that took hours or days to make was not abandoned lightly because an edge dulled or a fastening failed.

Archaeology often finds repair because repair leaves evidence: drilled holes near a crack, patches on clothing fragments, reshaped blades, reused metal, old buildings altered rather than demolished. These traces matter because they show use, scarcity, and intelligence after the first act of making. Human history is not only invention. It is maintenance: sharpening, greasing, darning, replacing, propping, cleaning, and adjusting what already exists.

For much of settled life, households contained repair capacity. Needles, awls, knives, wedges, hammers, cordage, glue, pitch, spare cloth, spare leather, and later screws and nails belonged to ordinary competence. Specialized repairers also mattered: cobblers, tinkers, blacksmiths, tailors, carpenters, wheelwrights, pot menders, watch repairers, and bicycle mechanics. Their work joined diagnosis with material judgment. They knew whether something was worth saving, which part carried the load, and where a neat fix would fail under stress.

The industrial age did not eliminate repair. It multiplied both the need and the means. Machines required lubrication, belts, brushes, seals, bearings, fuses, gaskets, and calibration. Railways, mills, farms, kitchens, ships, and factories depended on people who noticed wear before catastrophe. The glamorous story celebrated invention; the working story depended on maintenance crews, spare parts, manuals, and the person who heard a new sound in an engine before anyone else did.

03The Biology of Practical Skill

Fixing small things used the whole nervous system. The eyes inspected alignment, cracks, wear, color, and movement. The fingers felt burrs, looseness, heat, stickiness, roughness, and resistance. The ear heard a rattle, scrape, hiss, click, or change in pitch. The nose sometimes detected scorched insulation, gas, mildew, oil, or rot. Repair was perception sharpened by consequences.

Practical skill was partly procedural learning: the kind of learning that becomes embodied through repeated action. A person can read how to thread a needle, set a bicycle patch, or drive a screw straight, but the hand learns through doing. It learns how much pressure bends the needle, how long glue needs to become tacky, how a screwdriver cams out when the angle is wrong, how a nut feels when the threads are crossed, how a tire lever can pinch a tube if forced carelessly.

This kind of knowledge is often hard to explain because it lives in timing, pressure, and correction. Experts are not only people who know more facts. They are people whose senses have become tuned. A mechanic can feel play in a bearing. A tailor sees tension in a seam. A carpenter hears when a saw is binding. A parent who has fixed ten toys knows when plastic will accept a screw again and when it will crumble.

Repair also affected the mind. It developed patience with causes. It punished vague frustration and rewarded sequence: observe, test, isolate, loosen, clean, align, fasten, test again. It taught humility because objects do not care about excuses. A loose wire remains loose after a speech. A stripped screw remains stripped after anger. The world of things asks for contact with reality, and small repair is one of the most accessible ways to practice that contact.

04Repair Across Cultures

Every culture repaired, but cultures differed in how they made repair visible, honorable, or hidden. In many poor or resource-constrained settings, repair was not a lifestyle choice but common sense. A sandal was patched, a cooking pot was blackened and kept, a radio was opened, a shirt became a rag and then a tie and then stuffing. The life of an object stretched because money, material, and access were limited.

Mending traditions carried both necessity and beauty. Darning could be nearly invisible, returning a sock or sweater to service without drawing attention. Other forms of mending became visible: patches, quilting, piecing, reinforcement, sashiko stitching in Japan, and many local textile practices that used repeated stitches to strengthen worn cloth. These practices should not be flattened into one meaning. Some were born of poverty, some of aesthetics, some of household discipline, some of regional material culture, and often several at once.

Kintsugi, associated with Japanese repair of broken ceramics using lacquer sometimes dusted or mixed with powdered precious metal, is often invoked as a symbol of honoring damage. It deserves careful handling. It is a real craft with specific materials and history, not a universal slogan that every break is automatically beautiful. Its useful lesson is modest and strong: repair need not always pretend that damage never happened. Sometimes a restored object can carry its fracture honestly.

Repair cultures also appeared in public. Repair cafes, community workshops, tool libraries, bicycle co-ops, sewing circles, maker spaces, farm sheds, and neighborhood garages all preserved social repair knowledge. A person arrived with a toaster, coat, lamp, stroller, or bike and met someone who knew how to look. The repaired object mattered, but so did the transfer of courage. After watching one appliance opened safely, one button sewn back, one brake adjusted, a person became less helpless before the next small failure.

05What Thinkers and Traditions Noticed

Thinkers who studied craft noticed that making and fixing shape character. Richard Sennett described craftsmanship as a desire to do a job well for its own sake, and repair belongs inside that desire. Matthew B. Crawford argued for the moral and intellectual seriousness of manual competence, especially in a culture that often treats hands-on work as lesser than abstract work. The point is not to romanticize every repair bench. It is to recognize that material judgment is a form of intelligence.

Philosophers of care and maintenance noticed something invention stories often miss: keeping the world going is work. Roads, bodies, homes, machines, friendships, records, and tools all decay without attention. Maintenance is less dramatic than creation because it prevents the event that would have proved its importance. The hinge does not fall. The roof does not leak. The shoe lasts another season. Success looks like ordinary life continuing.

Religious and ethical traditions often praised stewardship, humility, and avoidance of waste, though they expressed these values in different languages. A repaired thing could become an education in gratitude: use what has been given, do not despise small labor, do not demand newness as proof of worth. Folk wisdom said similar things more bluntly: a stitch in time saves nine; measure twice, cut once; take care of your tools and they will take care of you.

Modern right-to-repair advocates added a legal and political insight. A person cannot repair what they are forbidden to open, cannot diagnose what software hides, cannot replace what parts are withheld, and cannot maintain what manuals are locked away. Repair is not only a personal virtue. It can depend on design, law, documentation, parts availability, and whether companies permit owners and independent technicians to service the things they bought.

06The 21st-Century Collapse of Repair

By the early 21st century, many people in wealthy societies had lost ordinary repair fluency. Objects became cheaper, more sealed, more electronic, more glued, more proprietary, and more quickly replaced. A broken button still needed only needle and thread, but the habit of reaching for them weakened. A loose hinge still needed a screwdriver and sometimes a longer screw, but the confidence to remove a screw without panic declined.

The collapse had several causes. Manufacturing made some goods inexpensive enough that repair seemed uneconomical. Global supply chains hid the labor and waste behind replacement. Design sometimes prioritized thinness, speed of assembly, waterproofing, or low cost over serviceability. Digital locks, parts pairing, unavailable schematics, unusual fasteners, and warranty fears made some devices harder to repair outside approved channels. Planned obsolescence is a real accusation in some contexts, but it should be used carefully: products also become obsolete through changing standards, fashion, software support, battery limits, material fatigue, and market pressure. The result, whatever the mixture of causes, was shorter attention to the life of things.

Education changed too. Many children grew up with fewer shop classes, fewer sewing lessons, fewer chores involving tools, and fewer adults repairing in visible ways. Safety concerns were sometimes reasonable, but the absence of guided risk left young people with no scale. A sharp needle, a hot glue gun, a utility knife, a battery terminal, and a gas line all became one vague category called dangerous. Without distinctions, fear replaced judgment.

Something emotional collapsed as well. People became ashamed of not knowing. Rather than ask how to sew on a button, they bought another shirt. Rather than admit they did not know how to patch a tire, they abandoned the bicycle. Consumer culture offered relief from embarrassment: buy new, upgrade, outsource, do not linger with failure. But lingering with failure is exactly how practical intelligence grows.

07How to Practice Fixing Small Things

Begin with harmless, common failures. The aim is not to become a licensed electrician, plumber, carpenter, tailor, and mechanic in a month. The aim is to become the kind of person who can look closely, use simple tools safely, and tell the difference between a small repair and a job that needs expertise.

Safety first Do not guess with electricity, gas, structural supports, major plumbing, cars, boilers, pressure vessels, or anything that could poison, burn, flood, collapse, or electrocute. Turn power off before basic electrical inspection. If you smell gas, leave and call the utility or emergency services. Use eye protection when cutting, drilling, or striking. Keep sharp tools sharp, controlled, and pointed away from flesh.

Beginner: build a small repair kit

  1. Gather a few reliable tools: scissors, sewing needles, thread, spare buttons, measuring tape, small hammer, adjustable wrench, pliers, screwdrivers, utility knife, sandpaper, tape, glue suited to the material, flashlight, work gloves, and a small container for screws.
  2. Fix a button. Match thread if possible. Anchor it, leave a little shank for thickness, wrap the stem, knot cleanly, and tug gently to test.
  3. Tighten a hinge. Support the door or cabinet, tighten loose screws, and if a screw hole is worn, fill it with a wooden toothpick and wood glue before replacing the screw after the glue sets.
  4. Patch a bicycle tube. Remove the wheel if needed, find the puncture, roughen the area, apply vulcanizing fluid if using a traditional patch kit, wait until tacky, press the patch firmly, check the tire for glass or wire, and inflate slowly.

Intermediate: diagnose before acting

Ask what changed. Did the object fail suddenly or gradually? Does the problem happen every time or only under load, heat, motion, or moisture? Which part moves when it should not? Which part does not move when it should? Clean the object first when safe; dirt hides cracks and makes false diagnoses easy. Take photos before disassembly. Put screws in order. Stop when you reach the limits of knowledge, tools, or safety.

Deeper: learn materials

Cloth tears along tension. Leather dries and cracks. Wood swells, splits, and holds fasteners with grain. Metal bends, fatigues, rusts, and strips. Plastic may flex, creep, crack, or refuse glue depending on type. Rubber perishes. Wire breaks at repeated bends. A worn shoe may need polish, glue, stitching, a new lace, an insole, or a cobbler. Repair gets better when the person stops thinking of all matter as generic stuff and begins seeing each material's habits.

08Common Failure Modes

Starting with force. Many beginners attack a problem before understanding it. Force has a place, but only after alignment, fasteners, hidden clips, adhesives, and load paths have been considered.

Using the wrong tool. A knife is not a screwdriver. Pliers are not a wrench. The wrong driver strips screws, the wrong glue fails, and the wrong needle damages fabric.

Skipping safety because the job is small. Small jobs still cut fingers, shock hands, burn skin, and launch fragments into eyes. Unplug, clamp, ventilate, protect, and slow down.

Repairing beyond competence. Pride is dangerous around electricity, gas, structural repairs, brakes, load-bearing parts, and anything involving fire or pressure. Calling a qualified person can be the most competent repair decision.

Confusing ugly with failed. A visible patch, careful tape wrap, or mismatched button may be perfectly good if it is safe, durable, and honest about its purpose.

Buying tools instead of practicing. Tool ownership is not tool literacy. A small kit used often teaches more than a wall of untouched equipment.

09Teaching Children Repair

Children learn repair best by seeing adults fix ordinary things without drama. Let them watch the button return to the coat, the chair leg get tightened, the toy receive new batteries, the shoe get cleaned and conditioned, the bicycle tire come off and go back on. Narrate the process plainly: first we look, then we test, then we choose the tool, then we try, then we check whether it worked.

Give children real but bounded tasks. A young child can sort screws, hold a flashlight, thread large beads, tape a torn paper page, wash a muddy tool, or choose a button from a jar. An older child can sew a simple patch, sand a rough edge, tighten a loose knob, oil a chain, replace a shoelace, or help find a puncture in a bike tube. The lesson is not only the repair. It is the discovery that matter responds to careful action.

Teach tool respect without theatrical fear. A needle is sharp in a specific way. A knife cuts along its edge. A hammer transfers force. Electricity can injure or kill. Gas can explode. A ladder can fall. These are not reasons to keep children helpless. They are reasons to teach distance, posture, supervision, power shutoffs, ventilation, protective glasses, and the sentence "I do not know enough to do this safely."

Let children see adults fail well. A patch may peel. A screw may strip. A repair may need to be undone and tried again. This is not humiliation. It is how the world gives feedback. Children who learn that failure can be inspected become adults less likely to throw away objects, projects, and sometimes relationships at the first sign of trouble.

10If Everything Broke

If supply chains, power, money, or services failed, repair would stop being quaint immediately. The useful person would not be the one with the newest objects, but the one who could keep old objects working: shoes dry, clothing warm, doors closing, carts rolling, bicycles moving, handles attached, lamps safe, water containers sealed, tools sharp, shelters patched.

In a hard situation, repair becomes triage. Fix what protects life first: warmth, water, shelter, sanitation, safe cooking, safe light, first aid, transport, communication. Do not waste energy perfecting a cosmetic repair while a roof leaks or a shoe sole opens. Salvage parts carefully. Label screws and components. Keep broken objects that contain useful fasteners, springs, cloth, cord, rubber, glass, containers, wire, wood, and metal, but do not let salvage become hoarding without order.

Tool literacy would become communal. One person might sew, another sharpen, another patch tires, another repair radios, another understand pumps, another know knots. The group that can teach these skills multiplies itself. Manuals, diagrams, handwritten notes, and labeled tool kits become part of survival memory. A repaired hinge may seem small until it is the hinge on the door that keeps weather, animals, or danger outside.

The deeper survival value is psychological. Repair tells frightened people that not every break is final. A torn coat can warm again. A dull knife can cut again. A loose wire, handled safely by someone competent, can carry current again. The future returns first in small functions.

11Further Reading

  • Elizabeth V. Spelman. Repair: The Impulse to Restore in a Fragile World.A philosophical exploration of repair across objects, bodies, relationships, and institutions.
  • Richard Sennett. The Craftsman.A broad account of craftsmanship, skill, practice, and the dignity of doing work well.
  • Matthew B. Crawford. Shop Class as Soulcraft.An argument for manual competence and the intelligence involved in practical work.
  • Aaron Perzanowski. The Right to Repair.A modern account of ownership, law, technology, and the politics of repair access.
  • David Edgerton. The Shock of the Old.A history of technology that pays attention to use, maintenance, and older technologies that continued to matter.
  • Tim Cooper, editor. Longer Lasting Products.Research on product lifetimes, durability, consumption, and policy around keeping goods in use.
