How to Be Bored

The strange human capacity to sit in empty time without immediately filling it: waiting rooms without phones, afternoons with nothing planned, children saying there is nothing to do, and the mind beginning to make its own weather again.

01What Boredom Was

Boredom was the uncomfortable space between wanting something to happen and discovering that nothing had volunteered.

It was not merely having nothing to do. A person could be surrounded by tasks and still be bored, because boredom had less to do with the number of available activities than with the felt absence of meaning, novelty, agency, or engagement. It was the child on the floor saying, “There is nothing to do,” while toys sat nearby. It was the adult in a waiting room whose hand kept reaching for a phone that had been left at home. It was standing in line, staring at the back of another person's coat, suddenly aware of one's own breathing, impatience, and small resentments.

Boredom was ordinary, but it was not trivial. It could be a warning that a life had become too narrow, a task too empty, a room too controlled, or a mind too underfed. It could also be the doorway to play, prayer, invention, memory, or rest. Much depended on whether a person fled it immediately or stayed long enough to learn what kind of emptiness it was.

Human beings often mistook boredom for evidence that something was wrong with the world. Sometimes the world was indeed wrong: a prison cell, a meaningless job, an isolated old age, a classroom built around humiliation and silence. But often boredom was not accusation. It was withdrawal from constant stimulation. It was the nervous system asking, in its clumsy way, what it should love next.

02The Deep History of Empty Time

For most of human history, empty time did not mean leisure in the modern sense. It arrived between necessities: while watching animals, tending fire, waiting for weather, nursing a child, grinding grain, mending tools, guarding a field, walking a road, or keeping company with the sick. There was often work nearby, but not every hour was densely occupied. People waited because life required waiting.

Before electric light, broadcast media, and pocket screens, the day had stronger edges. Night fell. Storms stopped travel. Winter slowed movement. Illness confined bodies. A letter took time. A visitor might not arrive. The mind had to inhabit stretches that could not be skipped. People filled some of this space with song, story, gossip, craft, prayer, games, sex, argument, and sleep. Still, silence and repetition remained close. The world had not yet been engineered to rescue every pause.

The word boredom is comparatively modern in English, but the experience is older than the word. Ancient and medieval writers spoke of tedium, weariness, melancholy, restlessness, and spiritual disgust. Monastic writers described acedia, a condition in which the monk became restless, listless, irritated by the cell, suspicious of his life, and eager for distraction. It was not identical to everyday boredom, depression, or laziness, but it recognized something familiar: the mind can become unable to remain where the body is.

Empty time also made culture possible. A shepherd carved a small object while animals grazed. A child invented rules for a stone, a stick, and a patch of dirt. People watched clouds, faces, fire, animals, and stars long enough to form patterns. Not every invention began in boredom, but boredom often cleared the ground where invention could begin. When nothing obvious presented itself, imagination had to become less decorative and more practical.

03The Biology of Boredom

Boredom is not one simple brain state. Psychologists often describe it as an unpleasant state of wanting to be engaged but failing to find a satisfying object for attention. It involves attention, motivation, arousal, meaning, and self-regulation. A bored person is not peacefully resting. They are usually agitated in a low-grade way: understimulated, restless, and aware that attention will not settle.

The body participates. Boredom can make minutes feel swollen. The chair becomes uncomfortable. The mind scans for escape. A person checks the clock, shifts position, opens a tab, reaches for a snack, starts an unnecessary argument, or refreshes a page that has no reason to change. This restlessness is part of boredom's function. It pushes the organism to seek a more rewarding, meaningful, or useful engagement.

Mind wandering is closely related but not identical. In boredom, attention may drift because the present task fails to hold it. In mind wandering more broadly, thought moves away from the immediate environment toward memory, fantasy, planning, rehearsal, regret, or imagination. Neuroscientists have cautiously associated internally directed thought with activity in what is often called the default mode network, a set of interacting brain regions active in many tasks involving self-reference, memory, future thinking, and social imagination. This does not mean the brain has a magic creativity switch. It means that when external demands loosen, the mind often begins working with its own material.

That inward work can be useful. Studies on mind wandering and creativity are mixed and depend on task, mood, and method, but ordinary experience supports a modest claim: some ideas need unpressured mental space. A solution arrives in the shower. A sentence appears while walking. A memory returns while staring out the window. The mind was not doing nothing. It was combining, sorting, simulating, and noticing beneath the surface of deliberate effort.

Boredom can also be risky. Chronic boredom is associated in research with impulsive behavior, substance use, problem gambling, unsafe driving, and poor well-being, though causation is complex. Boredom says, “Move.” Wisdom asks where. Without guidance, the push toward stimulation can become self-damage. With guidance, it can become curiosity, craft, conversation, study, repair, or rest.

04Boredom Across Cultures

Cultures do not all treat empty time the same way. Some honor rest as obedience, dignity, or wisdom. Some suspect idleness as moral danger. Some train children to sit quietly among adults. Others fill childhood with supervised activity. Some households let silence stand. Others rush to cover it with radio, television, talk, music, or chores. Boredom is felt inside a body, but the body has been trained by a culture.

Sabbath and rest traditions remind people that not all unproductive time is failure. Jewish Sabbath practice, Christian Sunday observance, and other religious rhythms have often protected time from ordinary commerce and labor, though communities have interpreted and practiced them differently. At their best, such traditions say that humans are not only engines of output. They need time marked off for worship, family, delight, memory, and release from control.

Monastic silence offered another kind of empty time. Silence was not mere absence of speech; it was a discipline for hearing what constant talk concealed. A novice might discover that the room was not the problem. The problem was the swarm inside: resentment, hunger, fantasy, fear, vanity, prayer, grief. Stillness exposed the person to themselves. This is why many people flee it.

Other forms of cultural boredom were harsher. Factory repetition, colonial confinement, domestic isolation, bureaucratic waiting, unemployment, and imprisonment produced empty time without freedom. The boredom of being able to choose nothing is different from the boredom of choosing not to be entertained. Any praise of boredom must keep that distinction. Voluntary boredom can deepen a life. Enforced emptiness can crush it.

05What Thinkers and Traditions Noticed

Religious and philosophical traditions often noticed that humans have trouble remaining with themselves. Blaise Pascal famously wrote in the Pensées about diversion and the human difficulty of staying quietly in a room. Different translations render the line differently, so it is safer to carry the idea than to pretend there is one final English sentence: people often seek noise, games, status, war, gossip, and entertainment because stillness reveals their fragility.

Schopenhauer treated boredom as one of life's central torments, paired with pain: when desire is frustrated, people suffer; when desire is satisfied and nothing presses, boredom appears. Kierkegaard wrote sharply and playfully about boredom, linking it to despair, rotation, and the restless attempt to keep life interesting by changing surfaces. Neither writer should be reduced to a slogan. Both saw that boredom was not merely a dull mood but a clue about desire, meaning, and the self's relation to time.

Artists noticed something less grim. Empty time could become a studio. A composer waited for a phrase. A poet stared at the same street until language loosened. A scientist walked away from a problem and later saw a relation that effort had obscured. Many creative people learned to protect idleness not because it always felt pleasant, but because a packed mind had no room to receive what had not yet announced itself.

Contemplative traditions noticed that the first layer of stillness is often boredom. Sit down to pray, meditate, or simply be quiet, and the mind complains. It wants better lighting, a different cushion, a snack, a message, a plan, a more interesting self. If the person stays, boredom may change texture. Under it can be sadness, fatigue, gratitude, anger, longing, or a plain and wordless sense of being alive. The practice is not to worship boredom. It is to stop treating every bored moment as an emergency.

06The 21st-Century Collapse of Boredom

The early 21st century did not eliminate boredom. It made boredom harder to reach in its older form. A person could still feel empty, restless, and disengaged, but the interval before stimulation became extremely short. The phone filled the cracks: elevator, bus stop, toilet, checkout line, red light, commercial break, restaurant table, bed, sidewalk, classroom, meeting, grief.

Smartphones changed waiting. A waiting room used to expose a person to old magazines, other bodies, wall paint, coughing, worry, and time. Now it often becomes a private tunnel of messages, headlines, games, videos, shopping, and feeds. This is not simply weakness. The device is useful, portable, socially required, and designed to answer boredom quickly. But the cost is real. The mind loses practice in passing through the first discomfort of empty time.

Children felt the change sharply. A child once said, “I'm bored,” and an adult might answer, “Good. Find something to do.” This was not always kind, and some children were neglected under the name of independence. But unstructured play gave children chances to invent games, negotiate rules, tolerate frustration, stare, wander, build, pretend, and discover preference. A child constantly supplied with screens and adult-designed activities may have fewer chances to meet the strange engine of their own imagination.

Adults also lost small solitudes. Walking without audio became unusual. Eating alone without reading became difficult. Standing in a line without checking anything felt almost rude to the modern nervous system. People became reachable, informed, entertained, and distracted. They also became less accustomed to being unoccupied, and therefore less able to tell the difference between true rest and mere consumption.

07How to Practice Boredom

Practicing boredom does not mean making life dull on purpose. It means recovering the ability to pass through unfilled time without immediate escape. Begin with small, ordinary scenes. Wait in a line without taking out the phone. Sit in the car for one minute before starting music. Look out a window while water boils. Arrive early and do not fill the gap. Let the mind complain.

Principle Boredom becomes useful when it is safe enough to stay, limited enough not to become despair, and open enough for the mind to find its own next movement.

Beginner: ten phone-free minutes

  1. Choose a low-stakes interval: a porch, a park bench, a waiting room, a kitchen chair, or a bus ride.
  2. Put the phone out of reach or leave it in another room. Do not make the boredom compete against a glowing escape hatch.
  3. Notice the first wave: irritation, twitching, planning, embarrassment, sleepiness, hunger, or the sudden need to check something.
  4. Do nothing heroic. Look around. Let thoughts wander. When an idea, memory, or feeling appears, notice it without immediately turning it into a task.

Intermediate: let a task ripen

Before solving a problem online, give it a little undistracted space. Write the question on paper. Take a walk without audio. Wash dishes. Stare out the window. Let the mind approach from the side. If nothing comes, then seek help. The point is not to reject tools. It is to give thought a chance to form before outsourcing it.

Deeper: schedule empty time

Protect a recurring block with no productive aim: a Sabbath-like hour, a quiet Sunday morning, a device-free walk, a chair by a window after dinner. Do not call it optimization. Do not measure it by breakthroughs. Some weeks it will be peaceful. Some weeks it will reveal exhaustion. Some weeks it will be boring in the flat, honest sense. That is still practice. A life with no empty margins has nowhere for the unplanned self to appear.

08Common Failure Modes

Romanticizing deprivation. Voluntary boredom is not the same as neglect, isolation, unemployment, imprisonment, or depression. Do not praise conditions that trap people in empty time without agency.

Confusing boredom with rest. Rest restores. Boredom often agitates. Sometimes the practice is to stay with boredom; sometimes the wiser answer is sleep, food, movement, companionship, or medical care.

Leaving the escape within reach. If the phone is in the hand, boredom may never fully arrive. The mind will spend the interval negotiating with the possibility of leaving.

Using boredom as punishment. Children and adults do not learn healthy stillness by being shamed into emptiness. Boredom needs enough safety to become exploration.

Expecting instant creativity. A bored minute may produce nothing but annoyance. Creativity is not a vending machine that accepts silence as payment. Empty time works slowly and irregularly.

Ignoring despair. If empty time consistently opens into hopelessness, panic, compulsion, or self-harm, the issue is not a lack of discipline. It is a signal to seek support from trusted people and qualified care.

09Teaching Children Boredom

Children need boredom in humane doses. A child saying “I have nothing to do” is not always making a complaint that must be solved. Sometimes the child is standing at the threshold of invention. If an adult rushes in with a screen, a purchase, or a fully planned activity every time, the child may learn that discomfort is always someone else's responsibility.

The answer is not abandonment. Children need materials, safety, time, and nearby adults who are not constantly directing them. Cardboard boxes, blankets, dirt, blocks, paper, crayons, water, sticks, dolls, balls, and ordinary household objects often invite more imagination than toys that do all the pretending themselves. Outdoor time helps because the world changes without being programmed: insects move, weather shifts, mud dries, shadows lengthen.

Adults can respond to boredom with calm confidence. “You may be bored for a while.” “You can read, draw, build, go outside, help me cook, or sit there until an idea comes.” “I am not going to entertain you right now, but I am nearby.” This teaches both boundary and trust. The child discovers that boredom is survivable and often temporary.

Screen boundaries matter most when they are predictable. A household that negotiates every bored minute teaches the child to become a lawyer. A household with clear device times, outdoor expectations, chores, books, and free play gives boredom somewhere to go. The goal is not a pure childhood untouched by technology. The goal is a child who can be alone with a thought long enough for it to become a game, a question, a drawing, or a conversation.

10If Everything Broke

If systems failed, boredom would return quickly. Batteries would die. Networks would vanish. Travel would slow. Information would arrive late. People would wait for water to boil, bread to rise, wounds to heal, weather to pass, messages to return, and repairs to hold. A group unable to tolerate boredom would become dangerous to itself, because boredom breeds foolish movement when stillness is required.

Practical survival contains long dull stretches. Watching a fire is boring until it is not. Keeping lookout is boring until one sound matters. Boiling water, drying clothes, tending a sick person, sorting supplies, walking the same route, and waiting for daylight all require attention without drama. The person who needs constant novelty may miss the small change that matters. The person who can be bored without fleeing becomes useful.

Empty time would also become social medicine. Without constant entertainment, people would need stories, songs, games, prayer, shared work, jokes, teaching, and silence. Children would need adults who could let them make their own play. Adults would need ways to sit with fear without turning every pause into conflict. A resilient community would not eliminate boredom. It would metabolize it into maintenance, watchfulness, imagination, and rest.

11Further Reading

  • Peter Toohey. Boredom: A Lively History.A readable historical account of boredom, tedium, and cultural interpretation.
  • Lars Svendsen. A Philosophy of Boredom.A philosophical treatment of boredom, modernity, meaning, and selfhood.
  • John D. Eastwood, Alexandra Frischen, Mark J. Fenske, and Daniel Smilek. The Unengaged Mind: Defining Boredom in Terms of Attention.A widely cited psychological account of boredom as failed engagement with attention.
  • Sandi Mann. The Upside of Downtime.A practical and popular discussion of boredom, mind wandering, and creativity.
  • Sherry Turkle. Reclaiming Conversation.Useful on phones, solitude, conversation, and the difficulty of being alone.
  • Peter Gray. Free to Learn.A strong argument for unstructured play, autonomy, and children's self-directed learning.
  • Jenny Odell. How to Do Nothing.A contemporary reflection on attention, resistance, place, and refusing constant productivity.
